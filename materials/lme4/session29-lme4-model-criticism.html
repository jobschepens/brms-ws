1 Introduction

Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs) and Generalized Linear Mixed-Effects Models (GLMMs) fitted via lme4 (Bates, Mächler, et al. 2015) are widely used in quantitative and experimental linguistics. They can model hierarchically clustered data, handle unbalanced designs, and account for variation across speakers and linguistic materials.

However, standard summary tables can hide assumption violations. A model that converges without error may still violate Gauss-Markov assumptions, have a degenerate random-effects structure (singularity), or be strongly affected by a few participants or unusual stimulus items (Meteyard and Davies 2020).

NoteDatasets

We apply some diagnostic workflows to three linguistic datasets from the ‘languageR’ package (Baayen, Feldman, and Schreuder 2006; Bresnan et al. 2007; Christensen 2019):

  1. Continuous Reaction Time LMM (languageR::lexdec) (Baayen, Feldman, and Schreuder 2006): Visual lexical decision reaction times (\(N = 1,659\)) with crossed random factors (21 Subjects crossed with 79 Words), continuous linguistic predictors (Lexical Frequency, Word Length), participant background (NativeLanguage), and experimental progression (Trial).
  2. Discrete Syntactic Choice GLMM (languageR::dative) (Bresnan et al. 2007): Dative alternation choices (\(N = 3,263\); double object NP give John the book vs. prepositional PP give the book to John), with predictors related to prominence hierarchies studied in CRC 1252 (Animacy, Pronominality, Givenness, End-Weight).
  3. Ordinal Subjective Rating CLMM (languageR::sizeRatings): Semantic size ratings (\(N = 3,078\)) on a 7-point Likert scale across 38 subjects and 81 concrete nouns, used to compare Gaussian and ordinal models.

2 Past 10 years

2.1 1. The Random-Effects Complexity Debate (2013)

In an influential paper, Barr et al. (2013) argued that confirmatory hypothesis testing requires “maximal” random-effects structures—including random intercepts and random slopes for all within-unit factors—to avoid Type I error rates rising to 20–40%. However, Bates, Kliegl, et al. (2015) and Matuschek et al. (2017) showed through simulations that maximal models often produce overparameterization, lower statistical power, and singular fits in finite datasets. They recommended data-driven model selection to balance Type I error control and power.

Note: For related simulation-based sample-size and power analyses, see the sequential testing notebook for condition effects, its continuous-predictor counterpart, and the interactive sequential-design sample-size report.

2.2 2. Simulation-Based GLMM Residuals: DHARMa (2016)

Diagnosing Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) is difficult because raw, Pearson, and deviance residuals are not easy to interpret for discrete binary and count outcomes. Florian Hartig (2022) introduced simulation-based quantile residuals using the Probability Integral Transform (PIT). DHARMa uses these residuals to test uniformity, overdispersion, and zero-inflation.

2.3 3. Ben Bolker’s Convergence Tooling & glmmTMB (continuously updated)

As lead maintainer of lme4, Ben Bolker helped develop tools for investigating optimization warnings (Bolker et al. 2009). Relevant tools include: - lme4::allFit(): Benchmarking models across multiple derivative-free optimizers to detect false-positive convergence warnings. - lme4::isSingular(): Mathematical boundary checking based on Cholesky factor singular values. - glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017): Flexible template-model-builder architecture handling complex dispersion, zero-inflation, and spatial/temporal autocorrelation structures. - trouBBlme4SolveR: Automated diagnostic routines to resolve boundary singularities.

2.4 4. Model Checks with easystats (2020)

The easystats packages (Lüdecke et al. 2021) provide tools for model diagnostics and summaries: - performance::check_model(): A visual overview covering linearity, homoscedasticity, posterior predictive checks, collinearity, and random-effect normality. Note: also see the brms posterior predictive checks notebook for Bayesian alternatives. - performance::r2_nakagawa(): Estimates marginal and conditional \(R^2\) (Nakagawa and Schielzeth 2013).

2.5 5. Marginal Effects (2016)

Russell Lenth’s emmeans (2016) and Vincent Arel-Bundock’s marginaleffects (2024) provide tools for post-hoc contrasts, reference grids, and response-scale predictions.

2.6 6. Bayesian Models with brms (2017)

Paul-Christian Bürkner’s brms (2017) provides an interface to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) estimation through Stan. As Vasishth et al. (2018) discuss, weakly informative priors (see Setting Priors in brms) can regularize boundary estimates while retaining scientifically motivated random slopes.

5.1 1.1 Random Effects Syntax

5.1.1 Overview:

Mixed-effects models relax the rigid balanced-design and sphericity assumptions of repeated-measures ANOVA:

  • Nested random effects (e.g., Students nested within Classrooms): (1 | Class / Student) expands to (1 | Class) + (1 | Class:Student).
  • Crossed random effects (e.g., Participants crossed with Stimulus Words): (1 | Subject) + (1 | Word)
  • Interaction operator :: (1 | Subject:Word) gives a random intercept for each observed Subject-Word pairing; use it when pair-specific variation is part of the design.
  • Correlated random intercept and slope: (1 + x | Subject) estimates their correlation as well as their variances.
  • Slope without an intercept: (0 + x | Subject) estimates a subject-specific slope for x without a subject-specific intercept; 0 + removes the intercept term.
  • Uncorrelated random intercept and slope: For numeric x, (1 + x || Subject) expands to (1 | Subject) + (0 + x | Subject), fitting the terms without their correlation. Compare (1 + x | Subject) (3 parameters) with (1 + x || Subject) (2 parameters). Fewer parameters make a singular fit less likely, but the model then assumes, for example, that subjects with slow baselines are no more or less sensitive to x than others.
  • diag(): the name of the same idea in glmmTMB, where a covariance structure is written as a function around the term: diag(1 + x | Subject) means a diagonal covariance matrix (variances only, zero correlations). Other choices there include us() (unstructured, the default |), cs() (compound symmetry), and ar1() (autoregressive). Plain lme4 has no diag(); there the double bar is the only shorthand for this structure.
WarningNote

|| only removes the correlations reliably for numeric predictors. If x is a factor with more than two levels, lme4 splits the term before it creates the dummy (contrast) columns, so the columns of the factor can stay correlated with each other. For factors, first create numeric contrast columns (as done below with Native_num and Sex_num) and use those inside ||, or write out the separate (0 + ... | ...) terms.

ImportantContrast Coding

In R, factors default to treatment (dummy) coding (contr.treatment), which maps the first alphabetical level to 0 and all other levels to 1. With interactions and random slopes, this coding affects how model coefficients are interpreted and can affect the fitted random-effects correlations (Schad et al. 2020):

  1. Misinterpretation of Main Effects: In models with interactions (\(A \times B\)), the lower-order \(\beta\) coefficients under treatment coding are not main effects; they are simple effects restricted specifically to the arbitrary baseline level of the other factor.
  2. Random Slope Correlations: In (1 + Condition | Subject), treatment coding defines the random intercept at the baseline condition. Depending on the data, this can increase dependence between intercept and slope estimates and contribute to correlations near \(\pm 1.0\) or singular fits.
  3. Type III ANOVA Tables: Type III tests (car::Anova(type = "III")) are often used with sum contrasts (contr.sum). With treatment coding, the tests may not answer the intended question.

Centered sum coding (\(-0.5 / +0.5\)) centers the intercept at the unweighted grand mean across conditions, decouples random intercepts from random slopes, and ensures \(\beta\) coefficients represent true main effects.

5.2 1.2 A Maximal Model (Barr et al., 2013)

According to Barr et al. (2013), any factor varying within a grouping unit must have a corresponding random slope: - Within Subject: Freq_z, Length_z - Within Word: Native_num, Sex_num

Toggle Code 
# Fit maximal random-effects model (Barr et al., 2013)
# Random intercepts and within-unit random slopes for all predictors:
# - Within Subject: Frequency and Word Length

m_maximal <- lmer(
  RT ~ Freq_z + Length_z + Native_num + Sex_num +
    (1 + Freq_z + Length_z | Subject) +
    (1 + Native_num + Sex_num | Word),

How to read these plots

  • Q-Q plot (left): If the residuals are normal, the points lie on the red line. Here the middle of the distribution follows the line closely, but the upper tail bends clearly away from it. A group of responses are much slower than a normal distribution predicts (a right-skewed, heavy upper tail). This is common in reaction-time data, even after a log transformation. The lower tail deviates only slightly.
  • Residuals vs. fitted (right): You want an even, structureless band around the dashed zero line. There is no obvious curve, so linearity looks adequate, and the spread is roughly constant across fitted values. The few large positive residuals are the same slow responses seen in the Q-Q plot.

Conclusion: The mean structure and variance look reasonable, but the heavy upper tail is a real departure from normality. With 1,659 observations, fixed-effect estimates are often fairly robust to this kind of non-normality, but standard errors and p-values can be affected, so check it with the simple follow-ups below.

Simple follow-up checks

  1. Count how many residuals are extreme (more than 2.5 standard deviations from zero).
  2. Check whether they cluster in particular participants or words. If they do, look at those clusters (see the influence diagnostics in Module 3).
  3. Refit without the extreme observations and compare the fixed effects. If estimates and conclusions barely change, the tail is not driving your results. If they change, report both models and consider a different transformation or response distribution.

6.2 2.2 Discrete GLMM Residuals

In discrete GLMMs (e.g. logistic regression), raw and Pearson residuals form parallel diagonal bands because outcomes are binary (\(y \in \{0, 1\}\)).

The DHARMa package (Hartig 2022) uses the Probability Integral Transform (PIT) to simulate \(n\) datasets from the fitted model and compute quantile residuals: \[u_i = P(Y^{\text{sim}}_i \le y_i) \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)\]

8 Module 4: Ordinal Models, Likert Ratings, and More QQ Plots

8.1 4.1 Likert Ratings

Linguistic experiments frequently collect ordinal ratings on Likert scales (e.g. acceptability, semantic size, prototypicality). Some analyses treat 1–7 Likert ratings as metric Gaussian variables using lme4::lmer().

This approach has three limitations: 1. Bounded Support: Data are bounded at 1 and 7 (floor/ceiling distortions). 2. Non-Interval Metric: The cognitive distance between “1” and “2” is not guaranteed to equal the distance between “4” and “5”. 3. Discrete Latent Thresholds: Responses reflect an underlying continuous latent variable \(Y^*\) partitioned by cutpoints \(\theta_1 < \theta_2 < \dots < \theta_{K-1}\).

9.2 5.2 Methodological Assumptions of emmeans

When reporting marginal means, consider these five points:

  1. Reference Grid Balancing: Continuous covariates are held constant at their grand mean (\(\bar{x}\)), and factor cells are weighted equally. When covariates are correlated, the reference grid can evaluate combinations unobserved in actual data.
  2. Scale of Inference: Differences on the link scale (log-odds) back-transform to odds ratios or ratio of medians on the response scale.
  3. Degrees of Freedom Approximations: For LMMs, emmeans supports Kenward-Roger and Satterthwaite approximations (Kuznetsova, Brockhoff, and Christensen 2017). Kenward-Roger is preferred in unbalanced crossed designs to avoid anticonservative tests.
  4. Homoscedasticity: Standard errors in emmeans assume a shared residual variance \(\sigma^2\) across factor levels.
  5. Multiplicity Corrections: When performing multiple contrasts, adjustments must be chosen deliberately:
    • Tukey HSD: All pairwise comparisons.
    • Bonferroni / Šidák: Planned orthogonal contrasts.
    • Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR): Large exploratory contrast families.

10 Module 6: Warnings & Reporting

10.1 6.1 lme4 Warnings

In practice, fitting complex mixed models frequently generates warnings. Understanding the exact mathematical origin of each warning determines whether it represents a critical model breakdown or a benign numerical artifact (Bolker et al. 2009):

10.1.1 1. Boundary Singularity Warnings (boundary (singular) fit: see ?isSingular)

  • Mathematical Cause: The parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) of the Cholesky factor \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\theta}\) lies on the lower boundary of the constrained parameter space. Specifically, one or more diagonal elements \(\theta_{ii} \le 10^{-4}\), meaning a random-effect variance has collapsed to zero or a correlation parameter has reached \(\pm 1.0\).
  • Substantive Causes:
    1. Overparameterized random effects (e.g. Barr’s maximal specification on datasets with few clusters or insufficient variation per item).
    2. Uncentered categorical or continuous predictors, forcing random intercepts to reflect arbitrary baseline cells rather than grand averages.
  • Diagnostic Protocol: Run isSingular(model, tol = 1e-4). Inspect VarCorr(model) for standard deviations of 0.000 or correlations of 1.000 / -1.000.
  • Remediation:
    1. Ensure all 2-level factors use centered sum coding (\(-0.5 / +0.5\)) and continuous predictors are standardized (\(z\)-scores).
    2. Use trouBBlme4SolveR::dwmw() to systematically detect and prune unidentifiable random slopes.
    3. Suppress random slope correlations using the double-bar syntax: (1 + x || Group).
    4. If maximal slopes are theoretically mandatory, switch to Bayesian mixed modeling with regularizing weakly informative priors (brms), which prevents boundary collapse (Vasishth et al. 2018).

10.1.2 2. Gradient Warnings (Model failed to converge with max|grad| = ... (tol = 0.002))

  • Mathematical Cause: The maximum absolute component of the estimated gradient vector of the deviance surface with respect to parameters exceeds the convergence tolerance (default tol = 0.002).
  • Why It Is Often a False Positive: The default tolerance in lme4 is conservative. Scaling disparities between predictors (e.g., reaction times in milliseconds \([400, 2000]\) vs. trial order \([1, 1000]\) vs. binary contrasts \([-0.5, 0.5]\)) can stretch the deviance surface into elongated, flat regions where gradient estimates oscillate.
  • Diagnostic Protocol:
    1. Extract the raw gradient and calculate the relative gradient using the inverse Hessian: \[\boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{H}^{-1} \mathbf{g}\]
    2. Run allFit() across multiple optimizers. If different optimizers yield identical log-likelihood values (within \(\Delta \log L < 10^{-4}\)) and fixed-effect estimates match to 3 decimal places, the warning is a false-positive numerical artifact and the solution is scientifically valid.
  • Remediation: Rescale predictors (\(z\)-scores); increase iteration count via lmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa", optCtrl = list(maxfun = 2e5)); or tighten tolerance (optCtrl = list(ftol_abs = 1e-8)).

10.1.3 3. Degenerate Hessian Warnings (degenerate Hessian with ... negative eigenvalues)

  • Mathematical Cause: The Hessian matrix \(\mathbf{H}\) (matrix of second partial derivatives) at the proposed optimum is not positive-definite, possessing zero or negative eigenvalues.
  • Severity: Critical. A non-positive-definite Hessian implies that the deviance surface has saddle points or flat ridges. Standard errors cannot be computed by inverting the Fisher Information matrix (\(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{H}^{-1}\)), rendering \(p\)-values and confidence intervals invalid or NaN.
  • Diagnostic Protocol: Compute eigen(m@optinfo$derivs$Hessian)$values. If \(\lambda_{\min} \le 0\), the Hessian is degenerate.
  • Remediation: Check for overparameterization or strong collinearity. Remove redundant fixed effects (high VIF) or simplify the random-effects structure.

10.1.4 4. Eigenvalue Ratio Warnings (Model is nearly unidentifiable: very large eigenvalue ratio)

  • Mathematical Cause: The condition number \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min}\) of the Hessian exceeds \(10^5\) or \(10^6\).
  • Substantive Cause: Ill-conditioning can result from scale differences between predictors, producing steep slopes in some directions and nearly flat regions of the deviance surface in others.
  • Remediation: Standardize all continuous predictors to \(z\)-scores (\(M=0, SD=1\)).

10.1.5 5. GLMM-Specific Warnings (pwrssUpdate did not converge in ... iterations)

  • Mathematical Cause: In glmer, the inner Penalized Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (PIRLS) algorithm failed to find a stable conditional mode \(u\).
  • Substantive Cause: Complete or quasi-complete separation (e.g. all trials in condition A resulted in 0 responses), extreme fitted probabilities (\(p < 10^{-5}\) or \(p > 1 - 10^{-5}\)), or complex random slopes in binary data.
  • Remediation:
    1. Switch optimizer to bobyqa via glmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa", optCtrl = list(maxfun = 1e5)).
    2. Check contingency tables for empty cells (table(data$Condition, data$Response)).
    3. Fit initially with nAGQ = 0 (fast Laplace approximation) to locate reasonable starting values.
    4. Transition to Bayesian estimation via brms::brm(family = bernoulli()) with weakly informative Student-\(t\) priors on \(\beta\), which can regularize complete separation without requiring cell dropping.

10.3 6.3 Likelihood Ratio Tests: Enforcing REML vs. ML Rules

  • Testing Fixed Effects Structures: Always use REML = FALSE (Maximum Likelihood). REML maximizes likelihood on a projected space orthogonal to fixed effects \(\mathbf{X}\); models with different fixed effects have different projection matrices, making LRTs under REML mathematically invalid.
  • Testing Random Effects Structures: Use REML = TRUE with anova(m1, m2) (fixed effects must be identical).
  • Final Parameter Reporting: Refit selected final model with REML = TRUE for unbiased variance component estimates.
Toggle Code

10.4 6.4 Variance Partitioning: Nakagawa’s \(R^2\)

For mixed models, standard \(R^2\) does not apply. Nakagawa & Schielzeth (2013) partition variance into: - Marginal \(R^2_m\): Proportion of variance explained by fixed effects alone. - Conditional \(R^2_c\): Proportion of variance explained by both fixed and random effects (total model variance).

10.5 6.5 Reporting Mixed-Effects Models

10.5.1 6.5.1 The Meteyard & Davies (2020) 10-Point Checklist

When submitting empirical mixed-effects analyses to peer-reviewed journals, follow this 10-point reporting standard:

  1. Software & Package Versions: State the exact version of R and all modeling packages (lme4, lmerTest, emmeans, DHARMa, performance).
  2. Data Preprocessing & Trimming: Document exclusion criteria (e.g. response time cutoffs, error trials), percentage of data trimmed, and centering/scaling transformations (\(z\)-score scaling with mean and SD stated).
  3. Contrast Coding Specification: Explicitly report factor coding schemes (e.g., centered sum contrasts \(-0.5 / +0.5\), treatment coding with baseline specified, or Helmert contrasts).
  4. Complete Model Formula Syntax: Provide the verbatim formula syntax for both fixed and random structures, clearly distinguishing between nested (/) and crossed structures.
  5. Convergence & Singularity Reporting: State whether isSingular() was tested, report the optimizer employed (bobyqa, Nelder-Mead), and state whether allFit() confirmed numerical stability.
  6. Inference Method & Degrees of Freedom: Declare the method used to compute \(p\)-values (e.g. Satterthwaite or Kenward-Roger approximation via lmerTest, or Likelihood Ratio Tests under ML).
  7. Fixed-Effects Parameter Table: Report unstandardized \(\beta\) coefficients, standard errors (\(SE\)), 95% confidence intervals, test statistics (\(t\) or \(z\)), and \(p\)-values.
  8. Random-Effects Covariance Architecture: Report standard deviations (\(\sigma\)), variances (\(\sigma^2\)), and correlation parameters (\(\rho\)) for all groupings, alongside residual standard deviation (\(\sigma_{\epsilon}\)).
  9. Goodness-of-Fit & Effect Sizes: Report Nakagawa’s Marginal \(R^2_m\) and Conditional \(R^2_c\), AIC, BIC, and log-likelihood.
  10. Open Science Deposit: Share reproducible Quarto (.qmd) source files, analysis scripts (.R), and de-identified data in public repositories (OSF, Zenodo, GitHub).

Results: Lexical Decision Reaction Times
Reaction times were log-transformed and analyzed using Linear Mixed-Effects Models fitted with lme4 (v1.1-35) in R (v4.4.1). Following Barr et al. (2013), an initial maximal model was fitted with crossed random intercepts for Subjects and Words, alongside random slopes for Frequency and Length within Subjects, and Native Language and Sex within Words.

Using automated singular variance pruning (trouBBlme4SolveR::dwmw()), non-identifiable random slopes were eliminated. The resulting parsimonious model included random intercepts for Subjects and Words, alongside a subject-level random slope for Frequency. Numerical benchmarking across eight derivative-free optimizers (lme4::allFit()) confirmed that all algorithms converged to identical log-likelihoods (\(\Delta \log L < 10^{-5}\)), verifying that the solution was stable and non-singular.

Fixed-effects significance tests were evaluated using two-tailed \(t\)-tests with Satterthwaite degrees-of-freedom approximations via lmerTest. Post-hoc estimated marginal means (emmeans) confirmed a substantial response disparity across language backgrounds (English: \(6.42\) log ms, \(95\%\text{ CI } [6.33, 6.51]\); Other: \(6.58\) log ms, \(95\%\text{ CI } [6.50, 6.66]\)). Overall variance partitioning (Nakagawa & Schielzeth, 2013) indicated that fixed effects accounted for \(18.4\%\) of total variance (\(R^2_m = 0.184\)), while the combined fixed and random effects explained \(47.2\%\) (\(R^2_c = 0.472\)).
